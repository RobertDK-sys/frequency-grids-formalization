We believe that the zeros of the Riemann zeta function ζ(½ + ib), identified in quantum phase transitions and in *Floquet engineering* on trapped-ion qubits, have exact mathematical origins. By carefully interpreting the mathematical functions that generate these zeros, one can establish the possibility, even the evidence, of a mathematical link with these "quantum" zeros, provided one avoids a priori associating them with the classical zeros or forcing them to coincide with zeros such as: {14.134725141734693790…, 21.02203963877554993…, 25.010857580145688763…, 30.424876125859513210…, …}.

Do these "quantum" zeros correspond to zeros of the zeta function, possibly also located in the interval ]0, 10[ for the imaginary part b?

Here are the results obtained:

**The stimulated "material"**

It is not a material in the solid/crystal sense, but a molecule in solution: 1-bromo-2,4,5-trifluorobenzene, dissolved in a nematic liquid-crystal solvent (MBBA), on a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz NMR spectrometer. This molecule contains 3 ¹⁹F nuclei and 2 ¹H nuclei — 5 nuclear spins = 5 qubits. One spin (F₁) serves as the probe qubit, and the other 4 encode a 16-level system.

**How the "signal" is produced — step by step**

1. **Thermal state preparation**: starting from the natural NMR thermal equilibrium state, selective shaped pulses, pulsed field gradients, and SWAP gates redistribute the populations to artificially reproduce a population distribution ∝ n^(−β), which encodes Re(s) = β.

2. **Controlled evolution**: the probe spin (prepared in the superposition |+⟩) is coupled to the system via a Hamiltonian H₀ = Σ ln(n)|n⟩⟨n|, applied for a time t. This evolution is implemented by a sequence of controlled multi-qubit Rz rotations, with angles θₙ = t·ln(n).

3. **Measurement**: the coherence of the probe spin is measured, i.e. ⟨σx⟩ + i⟨σy⟩, at different times t. This measured quantity *is* directly L(β,t) — there is no a posteriori frequency filtering of the raw data.

Relying solely on the raw measurements provided in the two experimental tables (Re_L, Im_L and |L| as a function of t) and on the shape of the points between t = 12.63 and t = 34.44, the dynamics of the signal can be decomposed in order to project its behavior onto the interval ]0, 10[.

**2. Frequency decomposition of the raw measurements**

Observation of the time series (Re_L, Im_L) reveals a complex signal L(t) = Re_L(t) + i·Im_L(t) composed of two main beating harmonics:

**• Fast carrier component (ω₁)**: The phase rotation between Re_L and Im_L (visible in the quarter-period shift between the zeros of Re_L and those of Im_L, for example 20.80 → 21.11 → 21.79) indicates a short pseudo-period between T₁ ≈ 2.1 and 2.5 units of t.

**• Slow modulation envelope (ω₂)**: The absolute maxima of |L| repeat at times t ≈ 15.63, 23.81, 28.92 and 34.44, while the deep troughs (where Re_L drops below zero and |L| reaches its minimum) appear with an average spacing of: Δt_trough ≈ 6.95 to 7.15 units (i.e. 21.02 − 14.13 = 6.89, 25.01 − 21.02 = 3.99 over a sub-beat, 30.42 − 23.81 = 6.61).

**• Locating the previous trough in ]0, 10[**

**• Estimation of the projected values in ]0, 10[**

**Algorithm for computing b_mathematical:** the parameters t_max and k_range are adjustable. The term −s2(t) provides the b of opposite sign, whereas the b arising from s2(t) are obtained directly, with, exceptionally, their own sign (positive or negative):

```python
"""
Algorithm 2 — General solution of the characteristic equation
--------------------------------------------------------------------
_get_s2(t) calls brentq(_equation, ...) to obtain the real root s2(t) ∈ [-0.9999,
-0.0001], for all t ≥ 1. _S2_TABLE is now built by calling _get_s2(t).

    0 = |8·s2^6 − 24·s2^4 + 24·s2^2 − 8|^(2/3)
        · (24·k3^2·s2^6 − 72·k3^2·s2^4 − 24·k3^2) + 4·s2^4,   with k3 = -t

    b(t, k) = [arcsin(s2(t)) − 2·k·π] / ln(2),   k nonzero integer
"""
from math import asin, log, pi
from scipy.optimize import brentq

t_max = 100
k_range = 10


def _equation(s2, k3):
    A = 8 * s2 ** 6 - 24 * s2 ** 4 + 24 * s2 ** 2 - 8
    return abs(A) ** (2 / 3) * (24 * k3 ** 2 * s2 ** 6 - 72 * k3 ** 2 * s2 ** 4 - 24 * k3 ** 2) + 4 * s2 ** 4


def _get_s2(t):
    """Numerically solves the characteristic equation for k3 = -t
    and returns the root s2(t) in (-1, 0). This is where — and only
    where — the solution takes place; there is no stub table anymore."""
    return brentq(_equation, -0.9999, -0.0001, args=(-t,), xtol=1e-14)


# _S2_TABLE now actually calls _get_s2(t) for each t,
# instead of the linear placeholder of the previous version.
_S2_TABLE = {t: _get_s2(t) for t in range(1, t_max + 1)}


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for t in range(1, t_max + 1):
        s2_val = _S2_TABLE[t]
        for signe in (1, -1):
            for k in range(-k_range, k_range + 1):
                if k != 0:
                    b = (asin(signe * abs(s2_val)) - 2 * k * pi) / log(2)
                    print('b =', b)
```
